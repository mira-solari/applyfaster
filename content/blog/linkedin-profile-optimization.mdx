Here's something that happens more often than you'd think: a hiring manager likes your resume, looks you up on LinkedIn, and sees a profile that tells a completely different story. Different job titles. A vague headline. An About section that hasn't been updated since 2021. Experience descriptions that don't match what's on the resume sitting in front of them.

It's not a dealbreaker every time. But it raises questions. And in a competitive job market, doubt is the last thing you want.

Your LinkedIn profile and resume aren't the same document, and they shouldn't be. But they need to tell the same story — same facts, same narrative, consistent picture of who you are professionally. Think of your resume as the tightly edited highlight reel and your LinkedIn as the extended cut.

Here's how to bring them into alignment, section by section.

## Your Headline: The Most Underused Real Estate on LinkedIn

Your LinkedIn headline is the single most visible piece of text on your profile. It appears next to your name in search results, in connection requests, in comments you leave on other people's posts, and at the top of your profile page. It follows you everywhere on the platform.

And yet, most people leave it as whatever LinkedIn auto-generated from their last job title. "Marketing Coordinator at Acme Corp." That's not a headline. That's a label.

Your headline should do two things: tell people what you do and signal the value you provide. It has 220 characters — use them strategically.

### What works

**Lead with the role you want, not just the one you have.** If you're a marketing coordinator looking to move into marketing management, your headline can reflect that aspiration while staying honest:

> Marketing Manager | B2B Content Strategy & Demand Generation | Driving Pipeline Through Data-Driven Campaigns

You're not lying about your current title (that's in your experience section). You're positioning yourself for where you're headed.

**Include keywords recruiters actually search for.** Recruiters use LinkedIn's search function extensively, and your headline is one of the most heavily weighted fields. Think about what someone would type into the search bar to find a person like you. "Project Manager" is a keyword. "PMP Certified" is a keyword. "Supply Chain Operations" is a keyword. "Passionate professional" is not.

**Use the pipe character (|) to separate concepts:**

> Software Engineer | Python, AWS, Microservices | Building Scalable Backend Systems

### What to avoid

- **"Open to Work" as your entire headline.** It signals desperation instead of competence. If you want to signal availability, use LinkedIn's "Open to Work" feature (the green frame), but keep your headline about what you bring to the table.
- **"Seeking New Opportunities."** Same problem. Your headline should describe who you are, not what you need.
- **Buzzwords without substance.** "Visionary Leader | Change Agent | Thought Leader" tells a recruiter nothing about your actual skills or industry. Be specific.

### Aligning with your resume

Your resume likely leads with your current job title or a professional summary. Your LinkedIn headline should use the same core language. If your resume positions you as a "Senior Data Analyst specializing in business intelligence," your LinkedIn headline shouldn't say "Data Guru and Analytics Enthusiast." Consistency matters — recruiters will check both.

## Your About Section: Tell the Story Your Resume Can't

The About section (formerly "Summary") gives you up to 2,600 characters to do something your resume doesn't allow: tell your professional story in your own voice.

Your resume is structured and formal. Your About section should be conversational. This is where you connect the dots between your experiences, explain what drives you, and give the reader a sense of who they'd actually be working with.

### The structure that works

**Opening line: A clear statement of who you are and what you do.** This is what people see before they click "see more," so make it count.

> I help SaaS companies turn underperforming sales teams into consistent quota-crushers through structured coaching programs and data-driven enablement strategies.

That's specific. It tells you the industry (SaaS), the function (sales enablement), and the impact (quota attainment). A recruiter searching for sales enablement professionals sees this and immediately knows whether to keep reading.

**Middle section: Your career narrative and key accomplishments.** This is where you provide context that doesn't fit on a resume — why you made a career move, how your different experiences connect into a coherent story.

Don't copy-paste your resume bullets. Weave them into a narrative. If your resume says you "increased retention by 22%," your About section might explain the insight that led to that strategy and why it mattered for the business. Include target keywords naturally throughout — LinkedIn's search algorithm indexes this section heavily.

**Final lines: What you're looking for (optional) and how to reach you.** If you're actively job searching, a brief note about what you're open to is perfectly appropriate at the end of your About section. Keep it professional:

> Currently exploring senior product management roles in health tech. Always open to connecting with others who are working at the intersection of technology and patient outcomes.

### Common About section mistakes

- **Leaving it blank.** A surprising number of people do this. An empty About section is a missed opportunity to control your narrative and improve your search visibility.
- **Writing in third person.** "John is a passionate marketing professional..." reads like a press release for someone who isn't important enough to have one. Write in first person. This is your profile, your voice.
- **Being too abstract.** "I'm passionate about leveraging synergies to drive transformative outcomes" means nothing to anyone. What do you actually do? For whom? What happens when you do it well?

## Experience Section: Consistent but Not Identical

Here's where the alignment between your resume and LinkedIn matters most — and where most people mess it up.

### Job titles should match

If your resume says "Senior Account Executive" and your LinkedIn says "Sales Leader," a recruiter might wonder which is accurate. Use the same titles in both places. If your company gave you a non-standard title (like "Customer Happiness Ninja"), use the official title on LinkedIn and add the functional equivalent in your headline or description.

### Dates should be consistent

This seems obvious, but discrepancies in employment dates between your resume and LinkedIn are one of the most common red flags recruiters check for. If your resume says you worked somewhere from March 2022 to November 2024, your LinkedIn should say the same thing. Rounding (listing just years) is fine if you do it consistently in both places.

### Descriptions can (and should) differ

Your resume bullets are tightly edited for space and optimized for ATS parsing. Your LinkedIn descriptions can be fuller. You have more room to explain context, describe the scope of your work, and add accomplishments that didn't fit on the resume.

Think of it this way: your resume includes your three strongest bullets per role. Your LinkedIn can include five or six, with more context for each. A hiring manager who's interested enough to check your LinkedIn is looking for additional evidence that you're the right fit. Give it to them.

That said, the core accomplishments should appear in both places. If your resume highlights that you "grew revenue by 35% year-over-year," that achievement should be on your LinkedIn too. If your top accomplishment only appears on one platform, you're creating a gap in your narrative.

## Skills Section: Match Your Resume's Keywords

LinkedIn lets you list up to 100 skills. Most people either add too many irrelevant ones or don't curate this section at all. Your skills section serves two purposes: it helps with LinkedIn's search algorithm, and it provides a quick-scan list for recruiters.

### How to align this section

Pull the key skills from your resume — the hard skills listed in your skills section and the competencies demonstrated in your bullet points. Make sure every important skill on your resume also appears in your LinkedIn skills section.

Then go one step further: look at the job descriptions for roles you're targeting. What skills do they list repeatedly? Add those to your LinkedIn skills section if you genuinely have them. This improves your visibility in recruiter searches.

### Pin your top three and seek endorsements

LinkedIn lets you select which three skills appear at the top of your skills section. Choose the three most relevant to your target roles. Having a few endorsements for those top skills — especially from people who actually worked with you — adds credibility. The easiest way to get endorsements is to give them first.

## Profile Photo: Yes, It Matters

Profiles with a professional photo get significantly more profile views than those without one. This isn't vanity — it's basic human psychology. People trust faces more than blank silhouettes.

### What constitutes a "professional" photo

- **Your face should fill about 60% of the frame.** Head and top of shoulders.
- **Good lighting.** Natural light works. Avoid harsh overhead fluorescent lighting.
- **Simple background.** A plain wall, outdoor setting, or office environment. Nothing distracting.
- **Professional attire** appropriate for your industry. If you work in finance, a suit makes sense. If you work in tech, a clean casual look is perfectly fine. Match the norms of where you want to work.
- **A genuine, approachable expression.** You don't need to grin like you're at a theme park. A slight, natural smile works. The goal is to look like someone people would want to work with.

### What to avoid

Cropped group photos (we can always tell), selfies, photos from five or more years ago that no longer look like you, sunglasses or hats that obscure your face, and overly filtered images. You don't need a professional photographer. A friend with a decent phone camera and good natural lighting can produce a perfectly adequate LinkedIn photo in ten minutes.

## Featured Section: Show, Don't Just Tell

LinkedIn's Featured section lets you pin posts, articles, links, and media to the top of your profile. Most people ignore it. That's a mistake, especially during a job search.

Use this section to showcase portfolio pieces, presentations, articles, project summaries, or even your resume itself. This gives recruiters tangible evidence of your work beyond bullet points.

## The Keyword Strategy That Ties It All Together

Think of your resume and LinkedIn as two versions of the same search-optimized document. The keywords that appear on your resume should also appear throughout your LinkedIn profile: in your headline, About section, experience descriptions, and skills list.

Here's a practical approach:

**Step 1:** Pull up five to six job descriptions for roles you're actively targeting.

**Step 2:** Identify terms that appear in multiple descriptions. These are your priority keywords.

**Step 3:** Make sure each of those terms appears somewhere in your LinkedIn profile — woven naturally into your descriptions, not crammed awkwardly into every sentence.

**Step 4:** Cross-check against your resume. If your resume features "supply chain optimization" but your LinkedIn never mentions it, you've left a gap. If your LinkedIn highlights "agile methodology" but your resume doesn't, the inconsistency could raise questions.

The goal is for someone to read your resume, then visit your LinkedIn, and think: "Same person, same story, same skills — just with more detail."

## Bottom Line

Your LinkedIn profile and resume should work as a team, not compete or contradict each other. Here's the checklist:

- **Headline:** Reflects your current positioning and includes searchable keywords that match your resume's core identity
- **About section:** Tells your professional story in first person, naturally incorporates target keywords, and explains the narrative behind your resume
- **Experience:** Same titles, same dates, same core accomplishments — with room for additional detail on LinkedIn
- **Skills:** Mirror your resume's key skills, plus keywords from your target job descriptions
- **Photo:** Professional, current, and approachable
- **Featured section:** Showcases tangible work that backs up your claims

Spend an hour getting these elements right, and you'll have a LinkedIn profile that reinforces everything your resume promises. When a recruiter checks — and they will — they'll find exactly what they're looking for.
