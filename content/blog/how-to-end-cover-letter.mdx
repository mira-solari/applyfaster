End your cover letter with a confident, forward-looking statement that reinforces your fit for the role and makes it easy for the hiring manager to take the next step. Not "I hope to hear from you soon" — that is passive and forgettable. Not a summary of your resume — they just read it. Your closing should do two things: remind the reader of your strongest selling point, and express clear interest in moving forward. Two to three sentences is enough.

That is the direct answer. But writing a closing that actually does those two things well — without sounding pushy, desperate, or robotic — takes more thought than most candidates give it. So let's break down exactly what works, what doesn't, and how to match your closing to different situations.

## Why the Closing Paragraph Carries More Weight Than It Should

Most candidates spend their energy on the [opening paragraph](/blog/how-to-start-cover-letter) and rush the ending. That is a mistake, because the closing is what the hiring manager is reading right before they make their decision. Psychologists call this the recency effect — the last thing someone reads tends to stick in memory.

Your closing paragraph also serves a practical function: it tells the hiring manager what happens next. Do you want an interview? Are you available to talk this week? Will you follow up? A cover letter that just trails off leaves the reader without a clear action to take, and when people are not given a clear next step, they tend to do nothing.

The good news is that strong closings follow predictable patterns. Once you know the formulas, you can adapt them to any role, industry, or career stage.

## The Closing Formulas That Actually Work

Every effective cover letter ending does three things in some combination: it restates your core value, it expresses genuine interest in the specific role, and it moves toward the next step. Here are the formulas, with examples you can adapt.

### Formula 1: The Confident Bridge

This is the most versatile closing. You connect one key qualification back to the role and express interest in discussing it further.

**The structure:** [Your strongest relevant qualification or accomplishment] + [how it applies to their specific need] + [forward-looking statement].

**Example for an experienced professional:**
> Managing a 12-person support team through a product migration taught me how to keep customers happy during periods of significant change — exactly the challenge your Director of CX posting describes. I would welcome the chance to discuss how that experience could translate to [Company].

**Example for a new graduate:**
> My capstone project involved building a predictive model for customer churn using the same tech stack listed in your job description, and I am eager to apply that work in a production environment. I would love to talk about how my research aligns with what your data team is building.

If you are a new grad and want more guidance on the full letter, our [new grad cover letter guide](/blog/new-grad-cover-letter-guide) covers everything from opening to close.

### Formula 2: The Enthusiasm-With-Evidence Close

This works well when you have genuine excitement about the company and can back it up with something specific. The key is pairing the enthusiasm with proof that you have done your homework.

**The structure:** [Specific reason you are excited about this company] + [what you would bring] + [invitation to continue the conversation].

**Example:**
> Your team's work on real-time fraud detection has been on my radar since you published that engineering blog post last fall — it is the kind of problem I spent three years solving at [Previous Company]. I would be glad to walk through how my approach to anomaly detection could contribute to what you are building.

**Example for a career changer:**
> Transitioning from classroom teaching to instructional design has been a deliberate move — I have spent the last year completing a certificate program and building a portfolio of e-learning modules. The ID role at [Company] is the exact intersection of education and technology that motivated this shift, and I would appreciate the opportunity to show you my work.

For more on making career transitions, our [career change cover letter guide](/blog/career-change-cover-letter-guide) walks through how to frame the full letter around your pivot.

### Formula 3: The Specific-Availability Close

This is the most direct formula, and it works best for roles where decisiveness is valued — sales, operations, startup environments. You state your interest and make yourself concretely available.

**The structure:** [Brief value statement] + [specific availability or next step].

**Example:**
> I am confident that my experience scaling B2B sales pipelines from $2M to $8M ARR is directly relevant to where [Company] is headed. I am available for a conversation any day this week and can be reached at [phone/email].

**Example for an internal move:**
> After three years on the product team, I have a deep understanding of our customer base and our technical constraints — context that would allow me to contribute to the partnerships team from day one. I would love to set up time to discuss this with you.

## What NOT to Do: Closings That Weaken Your Application

Some closing lines are so common that candidates assume they must be correct. They are not. Here are the endings you should avoid and why they hurt you.

### "I hope to hear from you soon"

This is the most common cover letter closing, and it is the weakest. "I hope" is passive — it positions you as someone waiting for something to happen rather than someone who makes things happen. It also tells the hiring manager nothing new. Of course you hope to hear from them. Everyone who applies hopes to hear back.

**Instead:** Replace hope with a concrete statement. "I would welcome the chance to discuss this further" is confident without being pushy. "I am available to talk this week" is direct and useful.

### Repeating your resume

> In closing, I have eight years of project management experience, PMP certification, and proficiency in Agile, Scrum, and Waterfall methodologies.

Your closing is not a summary section. The hiring manager just read your letter and has your resume in front of them. Restating your qualifications as a list in the final paragraph adds nothing — it just tells the reader you ran out of things to say. Your closing should move the conversation forward, not repeat what came before. If you are unsure what should go where, our guide on [what to include in a cover letter](/blog/what-to-include-in-cover-letter) maps out each paragraph's job.

### The Desperation Close

> I really need this job and would be so grateful for any opportunity to prove myself. I promise I will work harder than anyone else on your team.

Desperation is not a selling point. It makes hiring managers uncomfortable, and it signals that you do not believe your qualifications can speak for themselves. You do not need to beg — you need to demonstrate value and let the evidence do the work.

### The Ultimatum Close

> I will call your office on Monday to schedule an interview.

There is a fine line between confidence and presumption. Telling a hiring manager you will call their office to schedule an interview crosses it. You are not their peer yet — you are a candidate. Express availability and interest. Let them decide the next step.

### Apologizing or Undermining Yourself

> Although I may not have all the qualifications you are looking for, I am a fast learner and eager to grow.

Never end your cover letter by drawing attention to what you lack. If you are not fully qualified, the hiring manager will figure that out from your resume. Your cover letter's job is to make the strongest possible case for what you do bring. ## Matching Your Closing Tone to the Company

The right closing for a hedge fund is wrong for a creative agency, and vice versa. Tone matching is one of the subtler skills in cover letter writing, but it matters more in the closing than anywhere else because the ending sets the final impression.

### Formal and Traditional (Finance, Law, Government, Large Enterprise)

Stick close to professional conventions. "I would welcome the opportunity to discuss my qualifications further" works here. These environments expect polish, and a casual closing can read as careless.

**Example:**
> I believe my background in regulatory compliance and risk assessment aligns well with the expectations of this role. I am available at your convenience to discuss how I could contribute to [Company]'s compliance team.

### Conversational and Direct (Startups, Tech, Creative Agencies)

You have more room here. The closing can be shorter, more personal, and less formulaic. Avoid the stiff "I would welcome the opportunity" phrasing — it will feel out of place at a company where the job posting uses contractions and exclamation points.

**Example:**
> I have been wanting to work on developer tools since I first contributed to [Company]'s open-source SDK, and this role is the best version of that opportunity I have seen. Happy to chat whenever works for you.

### Somewhere in the Middle (Most Companies)

Most companies fall in the space between ultra-formal and startup-casual. When you are not sure, aim for professional but not stiff. Use the job posting as your guide — if it reads like a human wrote it, your closing can sound human too.

The overarching principle: your closing should sound like it was written by someone who would fit in at that company. If reading it out loud feels awkward or forced, it probably does not match the culture.

## The Often-Missed Step: Making Sure Your Closing Holds Up

Here is a problem that undoes a lot of otherwise strong closings: the candidate makes a specific claim in the final paragraph, and the resume sitting underneath the cover letter does not back it up.

Your closing says you are excited to bring your data engineering expertise to the role. But your resume still has your old generic summary from six months ago, and your most relevant data engineering project is buried in the third bullet of your second job entry. The hiring manager reads a confident closing, flips to the resume, and cannot quickly find the evidence. The disconnect creates doubt — and doubt is the last thing you want to leave someone with.

This is why [resume tailoring](/blog/resume-tailoring-guide) matters just as much as the cover letter itself. When your resume is structured to lead with the same qualifications your cover letter highlights, the closing feels credible. The hiring manager reads your final paragraph, glances at your resume, and the story checks out. That consistency is what turns a strong closing into an actual interview.

## Putting It All Together: Before and After

Here is an actual (anonymized) closing paragraph and a rewrite, so you can see the difference in practice.

**Before:**
> Thank you for your time and consideration. I hope to hear from you soon. I believe I would be a great addition to your team and I look forward to the opportunity to discuss my qualifications further.

**After:**
> Scaling a customer success team from 4 to 18 people while maintaining a 94% retention rate was the hardest and most rewarding work of my career — and it is exactly the kind of growth challenge your VP of CS posting describes. I am available to talk any day this week and would genuinely enjoy the conversation.

The "before" is 44 words of pure filler. It contains no information, no personality, and no reason for the hiring manager to remember this candidate. The "after" is 58 words with a specific accomplishment, a named metric, a connection to the role, and a clear next step. Same length, completely different impact.

## The Bottom Line

Your cover letter's closing paragraph is the last thing standing between your application and the "yes" or "no" pile. It does not need to be clever or long — it needs to be specific, confident, and forward-looking.

Restate your strongest selling point. Connect it to the role. Make it easy for the hiring manager to take the next step. Skip the filler phrases, the resume recaps, and the passive hoping.

Then — and this is the part most people skip — make sure the resume underneath your cover letter actually supports what your closing promises. The best applications are the ones where every piece reinforces the same argument: the [opening line](/blog/how-to-start-cover-letter), the body paragraphs, the closing, and the [tailored resume](/blog/how-to-tailor-resume-for-job) all pointing in the same direction.

Write a closing that gives the hiring manager a reason to pick up the phone. Then make sure the rest of your application delivers on it.
