Address your cover letter to the hiring manager by name. If the job posting lists who you'd report to, use that name. If it doesn't, spend five minutes on LinkedIn or the company's website to find it. If you genuinely cannot find a specific person after looking, use "Dear [Department] Hiring Team" — never "To Whom It May Concern," never "Dear Sir/Madam," and ideally not the generic "Dear Hiring Manager" either. That is the short answer, and if you follow it, you'll be ahead of most applicants before your letter even begins.

But getting the salutation right involves more than picking the correct words. It signals whether you did any research at all, whether you understand the company's culture, and whether this is a targeted application or one of fifty you blasted out today. This guide covers how to find the right name, what to write when you can't, the mistakes that quietly kill your first impression, and how addressing your letter properly sets up the rest of your application to land.

## Why the Salutation Matters More Than You Think

Hiring managers form an impression before they finish your first sentence. The salutation is the first thing they read, and it answers a binary question: did this person put in any effort, or is this a mass mailing?

A letter that opens with "Hi Sarah," tells the reader: I know who you are, I looked you up, and this application is for *you*. A letter that opens with "To Whom It May Concern" tells them the opposite — it's the cover letter equivalent of a cold call that starts with "Is this the homeowner?"

This matters because the salutation sets the tone for everything that follows. A personalized opening makes your first paragraph feel like a conversation. A generic one makes it feel like a form letter, even if the content is strong. You're fighting an uphill impression before you've made your case.

## How to Find the Hiring Manager's Name

Finding a name takes less time than people assume. Here are the methods that actually work, in order of reliability.

### 1. Check the Job Posting Itself

Start with the obvious. Some job postings name the hiring manager directly ("You'll report to Lisa Chen, VP of Engineering") or include a contact person for questions. Read the entire posting, including the fine print at the bottom. The name is there more often than you'd expect.

### 2. Search LinkedIn

Go to LinkedIn and search for the company. Then filter employees by the department the role sits in. You're looking for someone with a title one level above the position you're applying for. If you're applying for a Senior Software Engineer role, look for Engineering Managers or Directors of Engineering.

A few tips to narrow it down:

- **Filter by location.** If the job is in Austin, focus on team members in Austin.
- **Look at recent posts.** People who post about hiring for their team are often the hiring manager for that role.
- **Check who posted the job.** If a person (not the company page) shared the listing, there's a good chance they're involved in the hiring process.

You won't always land on the exact right person, but getting close is better than not trying. Addressing a letter to the Director of Marketing when the hiring manager is the Head of Content still shows more effort than "Dear Hiring Manager."

### 3. Check the Company Website

Look at the company's About page, Team page, or Leadership page. Smaller companies often list their entire team with titles. Even at larger companies, you can usually find department heads. Cross-reference with LinkedIn to confirm they're still in that role.

### 4. Call or Email the Company

This is old-school and it works. Call the company's main line, explain that you're applying for a specific role, and ask who the hiring manager is. Most receptionists will tell you. You can also email the recruiter listed on the posting and ask directly — "Could you let me know who the hiring manager is for this role? I'd like to address my cover letter to the right person." Recruiters are rarely bothered by this. It signals seriousness.

### 5. Check the Recruiter's Profile

If a third-party recruiter posted the role, they often know the hiring manager's name. Even if they won't share it, they may tell you the title or department, which narrows your salutation significantly.

## What to Write When You Cannot Find a Name

Sometimes there's no name to find. The company is large, the posting is vague, the team page is empty, and nobody picks up the phone. That happens. Here's how to handle it, ranked from strongest to weakest.

### Best Option: "Dear [Department] Hiring Team"

Examples:
- "Dear Marketing Hiring Team,"
- "Dear Engineering Recruiting Team,"
- "Dear Product Design Team,"

This works because it proves you at least know which department you're applying to. It's specific enough to feel targeted and general enough to be accurate even if multiple people review applications.

### Acceptable Option: "Dear [Job Title] Search Committee"

For academic, nonprofit, or government roles where a formal search committee is common:
- "Dear Search Committee,"
- "Dear Senior Analyst Search Committee,"

This is standard in fields where committees actually exist. In a corporate setting, it sounds slightly off.

### Last Resort: "Dear Hiring Manager"

It's not great, but it's not disqualifying. If your only alternative is guessing a name and getting it wrong, "Dear Hiring Manager" is the safer call. It's become so common that most hiring managers don't actively hold it against you — they just won't give you credit for effort, either.

### Never Use These

**"To Whom It May Concern"** — This belongs on a formal complaint to a utility company, not a job application. It's impersonal to the point of feeling hostile, and it signals that you have no idea who you're writing to and didn't care enough to find out.

**"Dear Sir/Madam"** — Dated, stiff, and assumes binary gender. There's no scenario where this is the best option. It makes your application feel like it was written in 1997.

**"Dear Sir," "Dear Madam," or "Dear Mrs./Ms./Mr. [Name]"** — If you're guessing someone's gender or marital status from their name, you have a meaningful chance of getting it wrong. Use their full name instead: "Dear Alex Rivera," or just "Hi Alex," depending on the formality level.

**"Hey!" or "What's up"** — Occasionally appears in startup applications. It doesn't land the way applicants think it does.

## Formal vs. Informal: Matching the Company's Tone

The level of formality in your salutation should match the company's culture, not your default setting. Here's how to read the room.

**"Hi [First Name],"** — Works for most tech companies, startups, marketing agencies, media companies, and any organization whose own communications use a casual tone. If their job posting says "we're looking for a rockstar" (cringe aside), they're not expecting "Dear Mr. Henderson."

**"Dear [First Name] [Last Name],"** — The safe middle ground. Professional without being stuffy. Works across virtually every industry and never reads as inappropriate.

**"Dear Mr./Ms. [Last Name],"** — Reserve this for law firms, banks, government agencies, academic institutions, and any setting where formality is part of the culture. When in doubt, read the company's website and job posting. If the language is formal, match it.

**The simple test:** Read the job description and the company's About page. Mirror their tone. If they write in first person and use contractions, "Hi Sarah," fits. If they write in third person and say "the ideal candidate shall possess," go with "Dear Ms. Thompson."

## Industry-Specific Conventions

Different fields have different norms, and getting this wrong can signal that you don't understand the culture you're trying to enter.

**Legal and finance:** Err on the side of formality. "Dear Ms. Nakamura," is the standard. Using first names in an initial cover letter to a law firm or investment bank can come across as presumptuous.

**Tech and startups:** Formality often works against you. "Dear Mr. Johnson" in a cover letter to a Series A startup reads as out of touch. "Hi Marcus," fits better.

**Government and education:** "Dear Dr. [Name]" if they have a doctorate. "Dear Professor [Name]" for academic positions. Getting titles right matters more in these fields than in any other.

**Creative industries:** Design firms, ad agencies, media companies — a warmer, more personal tone is welcome. "Hi [First Name]," with a personality-forward opening signals you understand the culture.

**Healthcare:** Title-sensitive. Use "Dr." for physicians. For administrative or non-clinical roles, standard professional conventions apply.

For internal applications — where you already know the hiring manager and the culture — the conventions shift entirely. Our [guide to internal position cover letters](/blog/cover-letter-internal-position) covers how to navigate the awkward dynamics of applying within your own company.

## Digital Applications vs. Printed Letters

The format of your salutation depends on how you're submitting it.

### Pasting Into an Application Portal

Most applications today are submitted through an online portal with a text box. In that context, skip the full formal heading (your address, date, company address) and go straight to the salutation. The portal already has your contact information. A wall of header text in a text box looks like you copied a Word document without thinking about where it was going.

Just start with:

> Dear Sarah Kim,
>
> [Your opening paragraph...]

### Uploading a PDF or Document

If you're uploading a cover letter as a separate file — or attaching it to an email — include a proper header that matches your resume's formatting. Your name, contact information, and the date should appear at the top, followed by the recipient's name, title, and company. Then the salutation.

**Visual consistency between your resume and cover letter signals attention to detail.** Same fonts, same layout structure, same contact block formatting. If you want a full breakdown of what belongs in every section, our [section-by-section cover letter guide](/blog/what-to-include-in-cover-letter) covers headers, body, closing, and sign-off.

### Email Cover Letters

When the instructions say "email your resume and cover letter," the email body *is* your cover letter. Use the job title and any reference number in the subject line, open with your salutation, and attach the resume. Don't attach a separate cover letter document and also write one in the email — pick one format, and the email body is almost always the better choice.

## The Salutation Is Step One. The Opening Paragraph Is Step Two.

Getting the name right earns you a half-second of goodwill. The opening paragraph is where you either cash in that goodwill or lose it.

The strongest cover letter openings do three things: name the specific role, reference something real about the company, and connect your experience to their needs — all in two or three sentences. The weakest ones ("I am writing to express my interest in...") undo whatever positive impression a personalized salutation created.

## Why Your Salutation Matters More When Your Resume Is Tailored

Here's the thing most cover letter advice misses entirely: your cover letter doesn't arrive alone. It lands alongside your resume, and the two documents either reinforce each other or send mixed signals.

When your resume is generic — the same version you send everywhere — a personalized salutation creates an odd contrast. "Dear Sarah, here's a letter clearly written for your company, attached to a resume that could be for any company." The hiring manager notices, even if they can't articulate why something feels off.

When your resume is [tailored to match the specific job description](/blog/how-to-tailor-resume-for-job), the entire application feels cohesive. The salutation signals effort. The opening paragraph builds on that signal. The resume confirms it. Every piece of the application tells the same story: this person targeted this role specifically.

That cohesion is what separates applications that get interviews from applications that get filed. ## Quick Reference: How to Address a Cover Letter

**If you know the hiring manager's name:**
- Casual culture: "Hi Sarah,"
- Standard professional: "Dear Sarah Kim,"
- Formal industry: "Dear Ms. Kim,"

**If you know the department but not the name:**
- "Dear Marketing Hiring Team,"
- "Dear Engineering Recruiting Team,"

**If you know almost nothing:**
- "Dear Hiring Manager," (last resort, not ideal)

**Never use:**
- "To Whom It May Concern"
- "Dear Sir/Madam"
- "Dear Sir" or "Dear Madam"
- "Hey!" or any ultra-casual greeting

**The five-minute rule:** Before defaulting to a generic salutation, spend five minutes on LinkedIn, the company website, and the job posting. That small investment separates your application from the stack.

## The Bottom Line

Addressing a cover letter correctly is the easiest part of the application to get right and one of the most common parts to get wrong. Use a name when you can find one. Use a department-specific alternative when you can't. Match the formality to the company culture. And never, under any circumstances, write "To Whom It May Concern" on a job application in 2026.

The salutation is a two-second decision that shapes how the rest of your letter gets read.
