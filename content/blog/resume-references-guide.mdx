# How to List References on a Resume (2026 Guide)

References are one of the most misunderstood parts of the job application process. Some candidates still put "References available upon request" at the bottom of every resume. Others include a full list of names and phone numbers on the resume itself. Neither approach is ideal in most situations.

This guide covers when references actually belong on your resume, how to format them correctly, who to choose, how to ask, and the mistakes that can quietly cost you opportunities.

## Should You Include References on Your Resume?

In most cases, no. You should not list references directly on your resume.

This might sound surprising if you were taught to always include them, but hiring practices have shifted. Here is why:

**Resume space is limited.** A resume should focus on your qualifications — skills, experience, accomplishments, and education. Every line dedicated to reference names and phone numbers is a line that could be showing a hiring manager why you are a strong candidate.

**Employers will ask when they need them.** References are typically checked late in the hiring process, after interviews and often after a verbal or conditional offer. Including them upfront means sharing personal contact information for your references before anyone has asked for it.

**Privacy matters.** Your references are real people who have agreed to speak on your behalf. Putting their phone numbers and email addresses on a document that gets uploaded to job portals, emailed around, and stored in databases exposes them unnecessarily.

### The Exceptions

There are situations where including references on your resume or alongside your application is appropriate:

- **The job posting explicitly asks for references.** If the listing says "please include three professional references with your application," include them. Follow instructions.
- **You are applying in academia or research.** Academic CVs commonly include references, and hiring committees expect them.
- **The industry norm requires it.** Some government positions, healthcare roles, and certain trades expect references as part of the initial application.
- **You are applying through a personal referral and the reference is the connection.** If a senior person at the company recommended you apply, mentioning that relationship (with their permission) can be strategic.

If none of these apply to you, keep references off your resume and prepare a separate reference sheet to provide when asked.

## Stop Writing "References Available Upon Request"

This line was standard resume practice for decades, but it is now considered outdated filler. Every employer already assumes you can provide references if asked. Writing it out does not add any information — it just takes up a line that could be used for something more valuable.

Removing this line will not hurt you. No recruiter has ever rejected a candidate for not including it. But keeping it can make your resume look slightly dated, which is the opposite of the impression you want to make.

## How to Create a Separate Reference Page

The best practice is to prepare a standalone reference document that matches the formatting of your resume. Keep it ready so you can provide it quickly when a hiring manager or recruiter asks.

### Reference Page Format

Your reference page should include:

**Your header** (matching your resume):
- Full name
- Phone number
- Email address
- LinkedIn URL (optional)

**For each reference, include:**
- Full name
- Job title
- Company name
- Phone number
- Email address
- Your relationship to them (e.g., "Direct supervisor at Greenfield Media, 2021–2023")

### Reference Page Example

```
ALEX CHEN
(555) 234-5678 | alex.chen@email.com | linkedin.com/in/alexchen

PROFESSIONAL REFERENCES

Sarah Martinez
Director of Operations, Apex Logistics
(555) 345-6789 | s.martinez@apexlogistics.com
Relationship: Direct supervisor (2022–2025)

David Park
Senior Product Manager, Elevate Software
(555) 456-7890 | d.park@elevatesoftware.com
Relationship: Cross-functional project lead; collaborated on 3 product launches

Rachel Okonkwo
Professor of Computer Science, State University
(555) 567-8901 | r.okonkwo@stateuniversity.edu
Relationship: Academic advisor and thesis supervisor (2019–2021)
```

A few things to notice about this example:

- The header matches what would appear on the candidate's resume, creating a cohesive application package.
- Each reference includes a brief description of the relationship, so the employer knows the context before making the call.
- The references represent different types of relationships — a direct manager, a cross-functional colleague, and an academic advisor — giving a well-rounded picture.

## How Many References Do You Need?

Three is the standard. Some employers ask for three to five. Having three strong references prepared is sufficient for the vast majority of job applications.

If you are applying for senior or executive positions, you may want to prepare four or five, ideally including:

- A direct supervisor or manager
- A peer or cross-functional colleague
- A direct report (if you have managed people)
- A client or external stakeholder (if relevant)

Quality matters more than quantity. Three people who can speak enthusiastically and specifically about your work are far more valuable than five who can only confirm your job title and dates.

## Who to Choose as a Reference

Picking the right references is a strategic decision. You want people who can do more than confirm you worked somewhere — you want people who can tell a story about your impact.

### Strong Reference Choices

**A recent direct manager.** This is the gold standard. Hiring managers want to hear from someone who supervised your work day-to-day. If your most recent manager is not an option (maybe you left on difficult terms, or they are no longer reachable), your second most recent manager works fine.

**A senior colleague or project lead.** Someone you worked closely with on a meaningful project can speak to your collaboration skills, technical abilities, and work ethic — sometimes more vividly than a manager can.

**A client or stakeholder.** If your work involves client-facing responsibilities, a satisfied client who can speak to your professionalism and results is a powerful reference.

**A mentor or professor.** For early-career candidates or career changers, an academic or professional mentor who knows your abilities and potential is a solid choice.

### Who to Avoid

**Family members.** Even if a relative is a professional in your field, the personal relationship undermines their credibility as a reference.

**Friends who happen to work at the same company.** If the relationship is primarily social, the reference will not carry weight. Choose someone who has directly observed your professional work.

**Anyone you have not spoken to in years.** A reference who barely remembers you is worse than no reference at all. If you have lost touch, reconnect meaningfully before listing them.

**A manager you had a difficult relationship with.** Even if they would not say anything overtly negative, lukewarm or hesitant responses from a reference can raise red flags for the employer.

## How to Ask Someone to Be Your Reference

Never list someone as a reference without asking first. This is not just courtesy — it is practical. A reference who is caught off guard by a phone call is less likely to give a polished, enthusiastic endorsement.

### The Ask: What to Include

When you reach out to a potential reference, cover these points:

1. **Let them know you are job searching.** A brief heads-up about your search gives them context.
2. **Ask directly if they would be comfortable serving as a reference.** Give them an easy way to say no. You want willing advocates, not reluctant ones.
3. **Tell them what types of roles you are targeting.** This helps them prepare relevant talking points.
4. **Share your updated resume.** This refreshes their memory on your accomplishments and gives them language they can echo.

### Example Email

> Hi Sarah,
>
> I hope things are going well at Apex. I wanted to let you know that I have started exploring new opportunities in operations management, particularly roles focused on process improvement and supply chain optimization.
>
> Would you be comfortable serving as a professional reference for me? I really valued working with you and think you could speak to my experience leading the warehouse consolidation project and the vendor management work we did together.
>
> I have attached my updated resume for reference. If any employers reach out, it would likely be in the next few weeks.
>
> Thanks so much for considering this. I completely understand if the timing does not work.
>
> Best,
> Alex

This message is respectful, specific, and gives Sarah everything she needs to say yes or no.

### Keep Your References Updated

Once someone agrees to be a reference, keep them in the loop:

- **Tell them when you have given their name to a specific employer.** Include the company name, the role title, and any details about what the employer might ask about.
- **Share what skills or experiences you would love them to highlight.** This is not coaching them to say something untrue — it is helping them prepare to tell the most relevant version of your story.
- **Thank them after the process, regardless of the outcome.** A brief note of gratitude goes a long way and keeps the relationship strong for future opportunities.

## Tailoring Your References to the Role

This is a step most candidates skip, but it can make a real difference. Just as you should tailor your resume to each job, you should think about which references are most relevant to each application.

If you are applying for a role that emphasizes leadership, prioritize the reference who managed you through a leadership experience or the direct report who can speak to your management style. If the role is highly technical, lead with the colleague who worked alongside you on the most complex technical project.

You do not need a completely different reference list for every application. But reordering your references or swapping one out based on what the specific role values can strengthen the overall impression.

This same principle of tailoring applies across your entire application. Your resume, your cover letter, and your reference list should all tell a consistent story that maps to what each employer is looking for. ## Common Reference Mistakes

**Listing references directly on your resume.** Unless the posting asks for them, this wastes space and exposes contact information prematurely.

**Not updating your references between applications.** If your reference gets a call and has no idea what company or role the caller is asking about, it reflects poorly on your preparation.

**Choosing references based on title alone.** A CEO who barely knows you is a weaker reference than a manager who worked with you daily for two years.

**Skipping contact verification.** Phone numbers change and people leave companies. Verify every number and email before submitting your reference sheet.

**Not preparing references for common questions.** Employers typically ask things like "What are their greatest strengths?" and "Would you work with them again?" Give your references a heads-up so they can think of specific examples rather than being put on the spot.

## Quick Reference Checklist

Before you submit your reference page, confirm:

- [ ] Three references who have agreed to be listed
- [ ] Each one knows what types of roles you are targeting
- [ ] All contact information is current and verified
- [ ] Reference page formatting matches your resume header
- [ ] Relationship context is included for each reference
- [ ] References are not listed on the resume itself (unless explicitly asked)
- [ ] "References available upon request" does not appear on your resume

## Final Thoughts

References are a supporting element of your application, not the centerpiece. But when handled well — the right people, properly prepared, provided at the right time — they reinforce everything else and can tip a close decision in your favor. Prepare your list early, keep it updated, and treat your references with the communication they deserve.
