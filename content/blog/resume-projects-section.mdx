# How to Showcase Projects on Your Resume (With Examples)

Not everything impressive you have done fits neatly into a "Work Experience" section. Maybe you built an app on your own time that is more technically sophisticated than anything you did at your day job. Maybe your capstone project is the best evidence you have of your design skills. Maybe you ran a freelance campaign that outperformed the work you did for your full-time employer.

A well-structured projects section can be the difference between a resume that looks thin and one that demonstrates real capability. Here is when to use one, how to format it, and what it looks like across different industries.

## When to Include a Projects Section

A projects section is not right for every resume. It earns its place when it adds information that your work experience alone cannot convey. Here are the situations where it makes the most impact:

**You are early in your career.** If you have one or two years of professional experience (or none), projects fill the gap between your education and your capabilities. A bootcamp grad with three strong portfolio projects looks significantly more hireable than one with just a diploma line.

**You are changing careers.** Your work history is in one field, but your projects show skills in the field you are targeting. A marketing professional who built a data dashboard in Python can use a projects section to bridge the gap to a data analyst role.

**Your side projects are more impressive than your job duties.** Sometimes your most compelling work happens outside of employment. An open-source contribution with hundreds of GitHub stars, a volunteer website redesign, or a personal app with real users — these deserve visibility.

**The job description specifically asks for a portfolio or project examples.** Many tech, design, and creative roles expect to see project work. If the posting mentions "portfolio" or "sample projects," a projects section is essentially mandatory.

**You have relevant academic or capstone projects.** Recent graduates can use significant school projects to demonstrate applied skills, especially if the projects involved real-world problems or external stakeholders.

## Where to Place the Projects Section

Placement depends on how central projects are to your candidacy:

- **Before work experience** — If projects are your strongest qualification (common for new grads, career changers, and bootcamp graduates)
- **After work experience** — If you have solid professional experience and projects are supplementary evidence
- **Integrated into work experience** — If the project was done as part of a job, include it as a bullet point under that role instead of in a separate section

For most job seekers, placing projects after work experience and before or after the education section works well.

## How to Format Each Project Entry

Every project entry should include four elements:

### 1. Project Name and Context

Give the project a clear name and one line of context. The context should immediately tell the reader what this is and why it exists.

**Format:**
**Project Name** — Brief description of what it is
*Technologies/tools used* | Date or timeframe

**Example:**
**BudgetTrack** — Personal finance dashboard for tracking spending across linked bank accounts
*React, Node.js, Plaid API, PostgreSQL* | January – April 2026

### 2. What You Built or Did (The Situation and Task)

In one or two sentences, explain the problem you were solving or the goal you were working toward. This gives the reader context before you describe your contribution.

### 3. Your Specific Contribution (The Action)

This is where most people fall short. They describe the project but not what *they* did. If it was a team project, specify your role. If it was solo, describe the key decisions you made and the challenges you overcame.

### 4. The Result or Impact

Quantify wherever possible. Downloads, users, performance improvements, grades, recognition — anything that shows the project achieved something measurable.

## Using the STAR Method for Project Descriptions

The STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) works just as well for project descriptions as it does for interview answers. Here is how to apply it in a compressed, resume-friendly format:

**Full STAR example for a resume bullet:**

"Identified that the team's manual QA process was causing a 3-day delay in every release cycle (Situation/Task). Built an automated testing pipeline using Selenium and GitHub Actions that ran 200+ test cases on every pull request (Action). Reduced QA cycle time from 3 days to 4 hours and caught 15 critical bugs before they reached staging (Result)."

On a resume, you would compress this into a tight bullet:

- Built an automated testing pipeline (Selenium, GitHub Actions) that reduced QA cycle time from 3 days to 4 hours and caught 15 critical bugs pre-staging

The STAR structure is still there — it is just compressed. The situation is implied (manual QA was slow), the action is clear (built the pipeline), and the result is quantified (3 days to 4 hours, 15 bugs caught).

## Tips for Making Your Projects Section Stand Out

### Always Include a Link When Possible

If the project has a live demo, a GitHub repository, a case study URL, or a portfolio page, include the link. Hiring managers — especially in tech and design — often click through to see the actual work. A live project with real users is dramatically more convincing than a description alone.

**Format:** Include the URL on the same line as the project name or technology stack.

### Focus on Your Contribution, Not Just the Project

For team projects, always clarify what you specifically did. "Built a mobile app with a team of 5" is vague. "Designed and implemented the payment processing module, integrating Stripe API and handling edge cases for failed transactions and refunds" is specific and creditable to you.

### Match Projects to the Job Description

You do not need to include every project you have ever worked on. Choose 2-4 that are most relevant to the role you are applying for. If a job emphasizes data analysis, lead with your analytics project, not your website redesign. If it emphasizes leadership, highlight the project where you led a team.

### Use Numbers Wherever Possible

Projects without metrics feel like homework. Projects with metrics feel like professional work. Compare:

- "Built a weather application using React"
- "Built a weather application (React, OpenWeather API) with 500+ daily active users and a 4.6 average rating on Product Hunt"

The second version demonstrates that someone besides you found the project valuable.

### Keep Descriptions Concise

Each project should take up 3-5 bullet points at most. If you find yourself writing a full paragraph for each one, you are including too much detail. Focus on what is most relevant to the job you are targeting and cut the rest.

### Do Not Include Trivial Projects

Tutorial follow-alongs, simple to-do apps, and basic calculator projects do not belong on a professional resume. The bar for inclusion should be: "Would a hiring manager find this interesting or impressive?" If the answer is no, leave it off. Two strong projects are better than five mediocre ones.

## When to Skip the Projects Section

A projects section is not mandatory. Skip it if:

- You have 10+ years of directly relevant work experience that already demonstrates your skills
- Your projects are not relevant to the role you are applying for
- The projects are too old to reflect your current abilities (a website you built 8 years ago using jQuery is not helping your React application)
- Including them would push your resume past two pages

In these cases, your work experience, skills section, and achievements should carry the weight on their own.

## Putting It All Together

A strong projects section tells a concise story for each entry: what you built, why it mattered, what you specifically did, and what the outcome was. It gives hiring managers concrete evidence of your capabilities — not theoretical skills, but demonstrated ones.

Whether you are a new graduate filling out a sparse resume, a career changer building a bridge to a new field, or an experienced professional showcasing side work that goes beyond your job description, the projects section is one of the most flexible and powerful tools on your resume. Use it deliberately, keep it relevant, and always lead with results.
