# 10 Resume Mistakes That Get Your Application Rejected by ATS

You spent hours perfecting your resume. You hit submit. And then — nothing. No interview, no rejection email, just silence.

There is a good chance a human never even saw your application. Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) filter out an estimated 70-75% of resumes before a recruiter lays eyes on them. And most of the time, the reasons are completely fixable.

Here are the 10 most common mistakes that cause ATS rejections, with concrete examples of what goes wrong and how to fix each one.

## 1. Using an Incompatible File Format

**The problem:** You submit your resume as a designed PDF exported from Canva, a .pages file, or an image-based format. The ATS either cannot parse it at all or scrambles the content into unreadable fragments.

**What happens:** The system extracts garbled text, misplaces your information into the wrong fields, or fails to extract anything. Your resume effectively becomes a blank page.

**The fix:** Submit your resume as a standard .docx file or a text-based PDF (one where you can select and copy the text). If you are unsure whether your PDF is parseable, open it and try to highlight the text with your cursor. If you cannot select individual words, the ATS cannot read them either.

**Before:** A beautifully designed PDF from a template site with text embedded in graphic elements.

**After:** A clean .docx file with the same content, using standard formatting. It will not win design awards, but every word is readable by the system.

## 2. Getting Creative With Section Headers

**The problem:** You name your sections something unique like "Where I've Made an Impact" instead of "Work Experience," or "My Toolkit" instead of "Skills."

**What happens:** ATS systems look for standard section headers to categorize your information. When the system cannot identify your work experience section, it may dump all your job entries into "additional information" or ignore them entirely. Your five years of relevant experience simply vanish from the parsed profile.

**The fix:** Stick with conventional headers:

- **Work Experience** or **Professional Experience** (not "Career Journey" or "My Story")
- **Education** (not "Academic Background" or "Where I Learned")
- **Skills** (not "Toolbox" or "What I Bring")
- **Certifications** (not "Credentials" or "Proof Points")
- **Projects** (not "Things I've Built")

Creative headers might impress a human reader, but they confuse the software that decides whether a human reader ever sees your resume in the first place.

## 3. Stuffing Keywords Unnaturally

**The problem:** You have heard that keywords matter, so you copy the entire job description's requirements section and paste it somewhere on your resume — sometimes in white text, sometimes in a "keywords" section at the bottom.

**What happens:** Modern ATS platforms are far more sophisticated than they were five years ago. Many now flag keyword stuffing as a red flag. Some use contextual analysis to check whether keywords appear in meaningful sentences or are just dumped in a list. White-text tricks are easily detected by systems that strip formatting and read raw text.

**The fix:** Integrate keywords naturally into your bullet points. If the job description asks for "project management," do not just list it — use it in context:

**Stuffed:** "Project management, project management software, project planning, project scheduling"

**Natural:** "Led project management for a 12-person cross-functional team, delivering 4 product launches on schedule and under budget using Asana and Gantt-based planning."

The second version contains the same keywords but in a format that passes both ATS parsing and human review.

## 4. Using Tables, Columns, and Text Boxes

**The problem:** Your resume uses a two-column layout, tables for formatting, or text boxes to position elements on the page. It looks great in Word or as a PDF.

**What happens:** Most ATS platforms read documents in a single linear stream, top to bottom. Tables and columns cause the system to read across rows instead of down columns, jumbling your content. A two-column layout where your name and contact info are in the left column and your summary is in the right column might get parsed as: "John Smith Marketing manager with 8 years | john@email.com of experience leading digital campaigns."

Text boxes are even worse — many ATS systems skip them entirely, meaning any content inside a text box simply does not exist in the parsed version.

**The fix:** Use a single-column layout. For spacing and alignment, use standard paragraph formatting, tabs, and line breaks — not tables or text boxes. If you want visual separation between sections, use horizontal lines created with the border function, not drawn shapes.

**Test it:** Copy your entire resume text and paste it into a plain text editor like Notepad. If the content reads in the correct order and nothing is missing, the ATS will likely parse it correctly.

## 5. Missing or Incorrect Contact Information Placement

**The problem:** Your contact information is placed in the header or footer of the document, or embedded in a text box at the top of the page.

**What happens:** Many ATS platforms do not read document headers and footers. They start parsing from the body of the document. If your name, email, and phone number are in the header, the system might not capture them at all. The recruiter sees a parsed profile with no contact information — and moves on.

**The fix:** Place your contact information in the body of the document, at the very top. Keep it simple:

```
Jane Martinez
jane.martinez@email.com | (555) 123-4567 | Denver, CO
linkedin.com/in/janemartinez
```

No header, no footer, no text box. Just plain text at the top of your document body.

## 6. Using Uncommon Fonts or Special Characters

**The problem:** You use a decorative font, or your resume includes special characters like bullets from symbol fonts, icons instead of text, or non-standard characters for visual flair.

**What happens:** ATS systems work with standard character sets. Unusual fonts can cause character substitution — your carefully formatted bullet points might turn into strings of random characters. Icons are either ignored or converted to meaningless symbols. Ligatures (where two characters are rendered as one glyph) can cause words to be misread.

**The fix:** Use standard, widely supported fonts: Arial, Calibri, Cambria, Georgia, Helvetica, or Times New Roman. For bullet points, use standard round bullets or hyphens. Replace any icons with text — instead of a phone icon next to your number, just write "Phone:" or skip the label entirely.

**Before:** A resume using Didot font with custom icon bullets and a star-rating system for skills.

**After:** The same resume in Calibri with standard bullets and skills listed as text with context (e.g., "Python — 5 years, production experience" instead of "Python ★★★★★").

## 7. Failing to Include Relevant Keywords From the Job Description

**The problem:** Your resume describes your experience accurately but uses different terminology than the job posting. You write "people management" when the job asks for "team leadership." You say "revenue growth" when they say "sales performance."

**What happens:** ATS systems match your resume content against the job description requirements. If the job asks for "Salesforce administration" and your resume says "CRM management," the system may not make the connection. You could be a perfect candidate whose resume scores poorly because of a vocabulary mismatch.

**The fix:** Read the job description carefully and identify the specific terms they use for required skills, tools, and responsibilities. Then use those exact terms in your resume — as long as they accurately describe your experience.

This does not mean copying the job description. It means translating your experience into the employer's language:

**Job description says:** "Experience with marketing automation platforms"
**Your resume before:** "Managed email campaigns using technology tools"
**Your resume after:** "Built and optimized marketing automation workflows in HubSpot, increasing email engagement by 28%"

Same experience, but now using the exact phrase the ATS is scanning for.

## 8. Listing Dates in Non-Standard Formats

**The problem:** Your dates are formatted inconsistently or in unusual ways: "Jan 2023 to Mar 2025" in one entry, "2022-2024" in another, and "Spring 2021 – Winter 2022" in a third.

**What happens:** ATS systems use date parsing to calculate your years of experience, identify employment gaps, and determine job tenure. When dates are inconsistent or use non-standard formats, the parser may miscalculate your experience or flag errors. Seasonal dates like "Spring 2021" often fail to parse at all.

**The fix:** Pick one date format and use it consistently throughout your entire resume. The safest formats are:

- **Month Year – Month Year:** "January 2023 – March 2025" or "Jan 2023 – Mar 2025"
- **MM/YYYY – MM/YYYY:** "01/2023 – 03/2025"

For your current role, use "Present" or "Current": "March 2024 – Present."

Avoid year-only formats ("2023 – 2025") when possible, as they strip away useful information and can make short tenures look like gaps.

## 9. Including Graphics, Charts, or Images

**The problem:** Your resume features a headshot, a skills chart showing proficiency levels as bar graphs, or infographic-style elements to make it stand out.

**What happens:** ATS systems cannot read images. Period. A pie chart showing that you spend 40% of your time on strategy and 60% on execution is invisible to the system. A headshot takes up space that could contain parseable text. A skills bar graph might look impressive to a human, but the ATS sees a blank space where your skills section should be.

**The fix:** Replace every visual element with text:

**Before (invisible to ATS):** A bar graph showing "JavaScript — 90%, Python — 75%, SQL — 85%"

**After (fully parseable):** "JavaScript (advanced, 6 years), Python (intermediate, 3 years), SQL (advanced, 5 years)"

If you want a visually impressive version for situations where you are handing your resume directly to a human (at a career fair, for example), keep a separate "human-readable" version. Your ATS version should prioritize parseability over design.

## 10. Submitting Without Testing Your Resume Against ATS

**The problem:** You finalize your resume, assume it looks fine, and submit it to dozens of job postings without ever checking how an ATS actually reads it.

**What happens:** You might be making one or more of the mistakes above without knowing it. Formatting that looks perfect on your screen might parse into chaos inside an ATS. You apply to 50 jobs and wonder why you are getting zero responses, when the issue is purely technical.

**The fix:** Before you start submitting applications, test your resume:

1. **The plain-text test:** Copy your entire resume and paste it into a plain text file (Notepad on Windows, TextEdit in plain text mode on Mac). Read through it. Does the content appear in the correct order? Is anything missing? Are there strange characters?

2. **The read-aloud test:** After pasting into plain text, read it from top to bottom. If the flow does not make sense — if your skills are jumbled into your work history or your contact info is missing — the ATS is having the same problem.

3. **The keyword check:** Compare your resume against the job description. Are the key required skills, tools, and qualifications present in your resume using the same terminology? Highlight matches. If less than 60-70% of the core requirements show up in your resume, you need to revise.

4. **The format test:** Try uploading your resume to a few job portals and check the preview. Many platforms (LinkedIn, Indeed, Workday) show you how your resume was parsed. If sections are misplaced or information is missing, you have a formatting problem.

## A Note on ATS Variation

Not all ATS platforms work the same way. Workday, Greenhouse, Lever, iCIMS, Taleo, and others each have their own parsing engines with different strengths and quirks. A resume that parses perfectly in one system might have issues in another.

This is exactly why the advice above focuses on the safest, most universally compatible approach. You cannot optimize for every ATS individually, but you can avoid the mistakes that cause problems across all of them.

## The Real Cost of ATS Mistakes

Here is what makes ATS mistakes especially painful: they are silent. You never get a notification that says "Your resume was rejected because our system could not read your two-column layout." You just never hear back.

That silence leads many job seekers to blame their qualifications when the real problem is formatting. They rewrite their bullet points, add more skills, tweak their summary — when all they really need to do is switch from a table-based layout to a single column, or move their contact info out of the header.

## Quick ATS Compatibility Checklist

Before submitting your next application, verify:

- [ ] File format is .docx or a text-selectable PDF
- [ ] Single-column layout with no tables, text boxes, or columns
- [ ] Section headers use standard names (Work Experience, Education, Skills)
- [ ] Contact information is in the document body, not the header/footer
- [ ] Standard font (Arial, Calibri, etc.) with no icons or special characters
- [ ] Dates are formatted consistently throughout
- [ ] No images, charts, graphs, or visual skill ratings
- [ ] Keywords from the job description appear naturally in bullet points
- [ ] Plain-text paste test shows content in the correct order with nothing missing

Fix these ten issues, and you dramatically increase the odds that your resume actually reaches a human recruiter. After that, your qualifications can speak for themselves.
