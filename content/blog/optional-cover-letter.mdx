Yes. If a job application says a cover letter is optional, you should include one. Every time. The word "optional" on a job posting does not mean "unnecessary" — it means "we won't automatically reject you without one, but we will absolutely notice if you send a good one." Skipping it when other candidates don't is handing away an advantage for free. And in a job market where most applications look identical, free advantages are not something you can afford to waste.

The rest of this article explains why that's the right call, what hiring managers actually think when they mark a cover letter as optional, the narrow situations where skipping might be defensible, and a five-minute framework for writing one that's worth sending.

## Why "Optional" Doesn't Mean "Unnecessary"

When a company marks a cover letter as optional, they're making a logistics decision, not a value judgment. There are three common reasons they do it:

**They don't want to scare off applicants.** Requiring a cover letter adds friction to the application process. Some companies have learned that making it mandatory reduces their applicant pool — especially for roles where volume matters. So they make it optional to keep the pipeline full. That doesn't mean they don't want to read one.

**They want to see who goes the extra step.** Some hiring managers use "optional" as a quiet filter. The candidates who include a letter are signaling something: they're willing to put in effort when nobody's forcing them to. That signal matters, especially in roles that require initiative, communication, or client-facing work.

**The HR team and the hiring manager have different priorities.** HR wants a streamlined process with maximum applicant flow. The hiring manager wants to find the best person. These goals sometimes conflict, and "optional" is the compromise. The cover letter field exists on the form because the hiring manager asked for it. It's marked optional because HR didn't want to make it a hard gate.

In all three scenarios, sending a cover letter helps you. In none of them does sending one hurt you — assuming the letter is actually good, which we'll get to.

## What Hiring Managers Actually Think When It Says "Optional"

The word "optional" lives on the job posting. The hiring manager's opinion lives somewhere else entirely.

When a hiring manager reviews a stack of applications for a role where the cover letter was optional, the applicants naturally sort into three groups:

1. **People who didn't send a cover letter.** These are evaluated purely on their resume. No penalty for skipping — but no additional context, no personality, no story beyond bullet points and job titles.

2. **People who sent a bad cover letter.** A generic template, an obvious ChatGPT output, or a letter that has nothing to do with the role. This is worse than sending nothing. As we've covered in our guide on [cover letter mistakes that get you rejected](/blog/cover-letter-mistakes-2026), a lazy letter can actively damage your candidacy.

3. **People who sent a good cover letter.** A short, specific letter that connects their experience to the role and sounds like it was written by a human with genuine interest. This group gets a real advantage — the hiring manager has context, a reason to be curious, and a sense of who this person is beyond their resume.

The math is simple. Most applicants fall into group one. A smaller number fall into group two. The handful in group three stand out — because they've done something most of their competition didn't bother to do.

This is especially true for roles at small and mid-size companies, where the hiring manager often reads every application personally. At those companies, your cover letter isn't disappearing into an ATS void. It's being read by the person who decides whether to call you.

## How Cover Letters Affect Your Chances

Let's talk about what the data actually shows — and what it doesn't.

You'll find plenty of articles citing specific percentages about cover letter impact: "26% more likely to get an interview," "83% of hiring managers read cover letters." Some of these numbers come from legitimate surveys. Some are recycled from studies with questionable methodology. We're not going to throw a specific number at you and pretend it's settled science.

Here's what we can say with confidence, based on consistent findings across multiple sources:

**Hiring managers at small and mid-size companies overwhelmingly report reading cover letters when they're submitted.** The smaller the company, the more likely your letter gets read by a decision-maker rather than being pre-screened by software.

**Cover letters have the most impact in competitive situations.** When the hiring manager has ten resumes that all look roughly qualified, the cover letter becomes the tiebreaker. It's the thing that makes one candidate feel like a person and the rest feel like paper.

**Tailored applications outperform generic ones across every metric.** This isn't a cover-letter-specific finding — it applies to resumes too. But the combination of a [tailored resume](/blog/how-to-tailor-resume-for-job) and a tailored cover letter performs better than either one alone. When both documents tell the same targeted story, you're making the hiring manager's job easy. They can see the match without having to infer it.

**Applications without cover letters are not penalized in most ATS systems.** The automated screen is about your resume's keyword match, not whether you attached a letter. But the ATS is only the first gate. The human review that follows is where the cover letter earns its keep.

The bottom line: including a cover letter doesn't guarantee an interview. But it consistently improves your odds, especially for roles where human judgment (rather than automated screening) drives the decision.

## When It MIGHT Be Okay to Skip

We said "always include one," and we meant it. But there are a small number of situations where the cost-benefit shifts far enough that skipping is defensible — not optimal, but defensible.

**Truly high-volume, entry-level roles with standardized hiring pipelines.** If you're applying to be a warehouse associate at a company that hires hundreds of people per month, your application is going through a system designed for volume. The process is resume keywords, background check, interview slot. Nobody is reading cover letters in that pipeline because the pipeline wasn't built for them.

**When the application explicitly says "do not include a cover letter."** This is different from "optional." If the posting says not to send one, don't. Following instructions is the baseline test, and failing it in the name of going above and beyond is still failing it.

**When you genuinely cannot write anything specific to the role.** If you know nothing about the company, haven't read the job description carefully, and can't articulate why this particular role interests you — then a vague, generic letter will do more harm than good. A bad cover letter is worse than no cover letter. But this scenario is a reason to do more research, not a reason to skip the letter permanently.

**When you're applying to dozens of roles per day in a volume strategy.** There's a [practical limit to how many jobs you can apply to per day](/blog/how-many-jobs-to-apply-to-per-day) before quality drops. If you're genuinely stretched thin and have to choose between a tailored resume and a tailored cover letter, the resume wins. But ideally, you shouldn't have to make that choice — which is where tools that speed up the process come in.

Notice that even in these exceptions, the answer isn't "cover letters don't matter." It's "in this specific situation, the effort might not be worth the return." For the vast majority of professional roles where the application says "optional," the effort is worth it.

## How to Write a Quick but Effective Optional Cover Letter

Here's the thing that changes the calculus: a good optional cover letter doesn't need to be long. In fact, it shouldn't be. You're not writing an essay. You're writing a short, targeted argument for why you're worth a phone call.

The [ideal cover letter length](/blog/how-long-should-cover-letter-be) is 250 to 300 words. For an optional cover letter, you can go even shorter — 200 words is fine if every word earns its place. You need three things:

**A specific opening.** Name the role. Say one thing that connects you to it or the company. Skip the "I am excited to apply" template language — it tells the reader nothing except that you're using a template.

**One or two proof points.** Pick your most relevant accomplishment and connect it directly to what the job description asks for. Use a specific story or result, not a generic claim. "I managed a team" says nothing. "I managed a team of six through a system migration that cut processing time by 40%" says something. For guidance on what to include, see our [complete section-by-section breakdown](/blog/what-to-include-in-cover-letter).

**A clean close.** Express interest, mention your availability, and stop. Don't grovel, don't over-explain, don't repeat your resume.

That's it. Three components, 200 to 300 words, and a clear connection between your experience and the role. If you want a deeper walkthrough of the full process, our guide on [how to write a cover letter in 2026](/blog/how-to-write-cover-letter-2026) covers everything from structure to tone.

## The 5-Minute Cover Letter Framework

If the reason you skip optional cover letters is time, this framework eliminates that excuse. Five minutes, four steps, one letter that's better than what 90% of applicants send.

### Minute 1: Read the Job Description With a Pen

Scan the job posting and underline three things: the job title, the most important qualification, and one thing about the company or role that genuinely interests you. You're not doing deep research. You're pulling the three facts you need to write a targeted opening.

### Minute 2: Write Your Opening (2-3 Sentences)

Combine those three facts into an opening paragraph. Name the role. State your relevant qualification. Mention why this company or role caught your attention. Keep it to two or three sentences.

Example: "I'm applying for the Senior Product Manager role at Meridian. I've spent four years shipping B2B products from zero to launch, most recently at a Series B startup where I owned the roadmap for a platform serving 200+ enterprise clients. Your focus on building tools for distributed teams is exactly the kind of problem I want to spend the next few years solving."

Three sentences. Specific. Connected. Done.

### Minute 3: Write Your Proof Point (3-4 Sentences)

Pick one accomplishment from your resume that maps directly to the job's key requirement. Describe what you did, the context, and the result. Be specific — numbers, outcomes, or tangible deliverables.

Example: "At my current company, I led the redesign of our onboarding flow after user research showed 35% of new customers dropped off within the first week. I worked with engineering and design to rebuild the experience over two sprints, and we reduced first-week churn by half. That project taught me how to balance speed with quality when the data is telling you something urgent."

Four sentences. One story. Directly relevant to the role.

### Minute 4: Write Your Close (1-2 Sentences)

Keep it simple. Express interest, mention you'd welcome a conversation, and stop.

Example: "I'd love to discuss how my experience building B2B onboarding systems could contribute to Meridian's growth. I'm available for a conversation whenever works for you."

### Minute 5: Read It Once and Send

Read the full letter out loud. If anything sounds like something you'd never actually say to another human, rewrite that sentence. Check for typos. Hit send.

Total output: roughly 200 to 250 words. Total time: five minutes. Total advantage over candidates who left the field blank: significant.

## Why Your Resume Matters Just as Much

A cover letter doesn't work in isolation. It works alongside your resume — and if your resume isn't tailored to the role, even a strong cover letter can't compensate.

Think about it from the hiring manager's perspective. They read your cover letter and think, "This person sounds like a great fit." Then they look at your resume and see a generic document that doesn't emphasize the skills they care about. The disconnect is jarring. It makes the cover letter feel performative rather than authentic.

The strongest applications are the ones where both documents tell the same story. Your cover letter says "here's why I'm right for this role" and your resume backs it up with the evidence arranged in the right order, using the right language, emphasizing the right accomplishments.

[Tailoring your resume](/blog/how-to-tailor-resume-for-job) means more than swapping keywords. It means reordering your bullet points so the most relevant ones come first, adjusting your summary to mirror the role, and making sure your skills section speaks the same language as the job description. When your resume and cover letter are both aligned to the same job, you're not just checking boxes — you're building a case.

This is where most applicants fall short. They might write a decent cover letter, but their resume is the same document they send everywhere. Or they tailor their resume but skip the cover letter. Either way, the application tells two different stories, and that inconsistency is visible to anyone paying attention.
